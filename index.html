Spartan1

Exact-settlement RFQ primitive on Permit2 · one contract · one function · zero trust added

A maker signs an order with exact amounts on both legs. It settles exactly as signed, or the whole transaction reverts. There is no partial fill, no slippage tolerance, no protocol custody, no upgrade path, and no stored state.

The name is the argument: every feature that is not essential to exact settlement has been removed on purpose, and the ones that were cut are named rather than quietly missing.

Don't take the claim — check it

Every field of the order is folded into one hash, and that hash is what the maker signs through Permit2. Change any single field and the signature stops verifying — not partially, not approximately. What it buys, and what it costs

Tighter on

  • Atomic exactness. Both legs move the signed amounts, checked on-chain after the transfers, or the transaction reverts whole.
  • Blast radius. Makers authorise one order at a time through Permit2. Spartan1 itself holds no standing allowance and no funds.
  • Surface. One file, one function, one library, one trust anchor. No owner, no proxy, no governance, no storage.

Deliberately not

  • Liquidity or routing. No oracle, no price discovery, no fair-value logic. It settles a quote someone else priced.
  • An aggregator. It is a primitive meant to sit behind wallets and frontends that already exist.
  • An everything-store. Batch settlement and cross-order matching are out of scope permanently, by decision rather than by backlog.

If you need deep liquidity and smart routing, use an aggregator. If you need a firm quote that executes exactly as signed or not at all, that is this.

One transaction, seven steps

  1. Static checks. Window inside the deadline, two different tokens, non-zero amount, real recipient.
  2. Derive the counterparty. An open order settles for whoever sent the transaction. It is never a free parameter.
  3. Read the window. Before it opens only the maker can settle. After, anyone can, and the tip is released.
  4. Snapshot balances. Both receiving sides, before anything moves.
  5. Pull from the maker first. Permit2 checks the deadline, the amount, the nonce and the signature before a single token of the caller's moves. An expired order costs nothing.
  6. Pay out. The exact signed amount to the counterparty; any surplus is the executor's tip.
  7. Prove both deltas, or revert. Each side must have received exactly what was signed. A fee-on-transfer token, a phantom token, a rounding surprise — all of them land here and reverse the whole transaction. This is where the guarantee actually lives.

Where it actually is

Not deployed · not audited · no capital

The contract and the off-chain layer are built and gated. Settlement against the real Permit2 on a live chain is still an open test, and the settlement address in the signing vector is still a placeholder that has to be re-frozen on deploy. No amount of passing tests replaces an independent audit, and the test gate is a precondition for one rather than a substitute.

The remaining risk is not mainly technical. A settlement venue needs a maker quoting and a taker arriving at the same moment, and that is harder to arrange than it is to build.
